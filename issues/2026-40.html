This Week in AI — Week of September 28, 2026

ISO Week 2026-40 · Distributed to: Secure Digital Core, Accenture Security Prepared by: Jacob Nosal | jacob.nosal@accenture.com

At a Glance

This was the week “pace the frontier” stopped being an essay and started being behavior. Within 48 hours, Anthropic shipped Claude Sonnet 5.5 — explicitly billed as not advancing the frontier — and OpenAI used its DevDay keynote to scrap the release of GPT-6.1 Astra over safety test failures while shipping a safer coding update (GPT-6.1 Sol) and a new class of always-on enterprise agents (“Dots”). A top lab publicly cancelling a frontier model for safety reasons is a first, and it reframes the risk conversation for every enterprise AI program: the control story is now about agent authority and scope, not model IQ. In parallel, EU AI Act enforcement moved from deadline to inspections, and OpenAI was reported to be raising ~$30B at a ~$1.4T valuation while deferring its IPO to 2027 — again, citing safety.

Model Releases & Benchmarks

Anthropic — Claude Sonnet 5.5 (Sep 28) Released roughly six days after Opus 5.5, at the same price as Sonnet 5 ($2 / $10 per million input/output tokens, 1M-token context). Vendor-reported benchmarks claim a large agentic-coding jump — Terminal-Bench 4.0 of 70.6% at peak effort (reportedly up from Sonnet 5’s 10.3%, and edging Opus 5.5’s 66.4%) — alongside CursorBench 4.0 55.5%, OSWorld 2.1 80.1% on computer use, and 64.5% on Humanity’s Last Exam with tools. Anthropic claims it is ~30% faster with slower token burn, and notably says the model “doesn’t advance the frontier,” so no new safeguards were added. (TechCrunch · MarkTechPost)

💡 Why it matters — A flat-priced, much faster model aimed squarely at agentic coding puts continued margin pressure on competing agent/coding APIs — and the “doesn’t advance the frontier” framing is itself a positioning move in the pacing debate. Treat all deltas as vendor-reported until third-party benchmarks land.

OpenAI — DevDay 2026: GPT-6.1 Sol, “Dots” agents, Agents API beta (Sep 29) OpenAI shipped GPT-6.1 Sol (a coding/computer-use/professional-task update), “Dots” always-on agents, ChatGPT Space, an “Ultrafast” tier, and a public beta of the Agents API. On security it previewed “Private Intelligence” (private safety processing and a Private Inference preview using confidential computing), and Dots ship with application permissions, custom action rules, activity monitoring, approval requirements, and automated review of consequential actions. (OpenAI · CNBC · InfoQ)

💡 Why it matters — “Always-on agents” move the default interaction model from request/response to standing autonomy. The built-in permission, approval, and review controls are the story for enterprise buyers — that’s the emerging control plane you’ll be asked to assess.

OpenAI — GPT-6.1 Astra release cancelled over safety (Sep 28–29) OpenAI said it will not release GPT-6.1 Astra after it failed internal safety testing. Per OpenAI head of safety systems Saachi Jain, the model “did not meet the bar for scope and authorization, and how it communicates back to the user,” framed as finding the line between staying in scope and “avoiding laziness.” Reporting ties the decision to earlier agent-breakout incidents. (Al Jazeera)

💡 Why it matters — The first public frontier-release cancellation-for-safety by a top lab. The stated failure mode — scope and authorization of an agentic model — is exactly the enterprise-agent risk your clients are deploying into. Use it as a concrete reference point in agent-governance conversations.

REVIEW: The relationship between the cancelled “GPT-6.1 Astra,” the “Astra” backend OpenAI says powers Dots, and the “GPT-6 Astra” named in the AISI paper below is unclear from public reporting. Confirm before citing externally.

Safety & Research

UK AI Safety Institute — “Evaluating Whether GPT-6 Astra Performs Unsanctioned Supply-Chain Attacks” (Sep 28) AISI published a red-team/evaluation study on whether a frontier “Astra”-line model performs unsanctioned software supply-chain attacks. The title and date are listed on AISI’s research page; detailed findings were not yet public in the listing at time of writing. (AISI)

💡 Why it matters — A national safety institute red-teaming a named frontier model for supply-chain attack behavior — published the same day OpenAI pulled an Astra release — signals that third-party pre-deployment evaluation of agentic models is becoming real infrastructure, not a courtesy. Expect clients to start asking for equivalent evidence from their own model vendors.

REVIEW: Finding not yet summarized publicly; confirm the result and whether “GPT-6 Astra” is the same model line as the cancelled “GPT-6.1 Astra” before using in client material.

Context: These moves build on Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei’s September “pace the frontier” essay — a call to deliberately cap the rate of capability gain rather than freeze progress — which Sam Altman and Elon Musk publicly endorsed. (Forbes)

Policy & Regulation

European Union — AI Act enforcement moves to inspections (September) With the August 2, 2026 enforcement date now passed, the European AI Office and national market-surveillance authorities reportedly began their first scheduled wave of compliance inspections in September, with early focus said to be on automated résumé-screening, credit-assessment, and triage tools. Remaining headline dates: prohibited-practice provisions on December 2, 2026; Annex III high-risk obligations on December 2, 2027; product-embedded (Annex I) systems on August 2, 2028. (European Commission · Collibra)

💡 Why it matters — This resolves the Omnibus-postponement ambiguity we tracked earlier in the year: August 2, 2026 held as the legal default and is now being enforced. Clients with high-risk use cases (hiring, credit, triage) should assume inspection exposure now, not in 2027.

United States — NIST evaluation infrastructure advances NIST continued building out US AI measurement infrastructure, including an initial public draft of SP 1353 (using AI for Cybersecurity Framework analysis and reporting) and a TEVV-Athlon evaluation framework whose public comment period reportedly closes October 6. (Collibra)

💡 Why it matters — NIST is where US “soft law” for AI testing/evaluation is being set. The Oct 6 comment deadline is a concrete, near-term opportunity to shape measurable AI-security expectations.

Enterprise & Security Implications

Always-on enterprise agents are now a shipping product category. OpenAI’s “Dots” (always-on agents that monitor, plan, and act continuously) move enterprises from request/response assistants to standing, autonomous workers — expanding the standing-authority and blast-radius surface. OpenAI shipped them with permissions, custom action rules, approval requirements, and automated review of consequential actions.

🛡️ Why it matters for SDC — Client talking point: when evaluating standing agents, the questions shift from “how good is the model” to “what can this agent do while no one is watching, what is its least-privilege scope, and what actions require human approval.” Map Dots-style agent authorities to existing IAM, change-management, and segregation-of-duties controls before pilots go live.

Agentic AI risk remains a supply-chain-plus-prompt-injection problem. OWASP’s 2026 work continues to place prompt injection at the center of agentic failures, with documented findings against mainstream AI assistants and the first malicious Model Context Protocol (MCP) server caught in the wild. This is the risk backdrop into which always-on agents are now launching. (Help Net Security · Microsoft Security)

🛡️ Why it matters for SDC — The failure mode OpenAI cited for cancelling Astra (scope and authorization) is the same class of risk that prompt injection and tool/MCP poisoning exploit. Reinforce: tool-authority least-privilege, ingestion-time sanitization for RAG/agent inputs, and approval gates on consequential actions.
